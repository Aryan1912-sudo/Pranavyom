How ZERO-GRAM works

A daily health companion for astronauts on a 450-day Mars mission, when Earth is up to 22 minutes away by radio. Here is every rule behind the numbers and colours.

01 · The ideaScreen-based checks, no extra hardware

A Mars crew cannot wait for a doctor on Earth. ZERO-GRAM turns a tablet into a 3–4 minute daily check-in covering the systems spaceflight harms most: brain speed, eyes (SANS), memory, muscle and bone, hydration, and mood and sleep. Every result is compared with that astronaut's own normal, then explained in plain words.

02 · The daily check-inSix tests

Heart rate, SpO₂, sleep, cabin air and radiation come from wearables and cabin sensors; in this prototype they are simulated.

03 · Personal normal & traffic lightsComparing each astronaut with themselves

Each astronaut has a pre-flight baseline: usual value (μ) and normal spread (σ). Today's value becomes a Z-score; only the unhealthy direction counts.

Z = (today's value − personal mean) ÷ personal spread

● All good below 1.5σ · ● Keep an eye 1.5σ–2.5σ · ● Act now 2.5σ or more. Every status also has a word, never colour alone.

05 · Mission health scoreOne number from 0 to 100

  • Each system scores 0–100: 100 − 12 × Z while green, 82 − 40 × (Z − 1.5) when yellow, 30 when red; fixed limits give 100 / 58 / 30. Minus 6 per extra warning.
  • Weights: Heart 20% · Radiation 20% · Mind 15% · Eyes 15% · Immune 10% · Sleep 10% · Bones & muscle 10%.
Score = weighted average − 9 × Yellow systems − 15 × Red systems Any Yellow → at most 89 · Any Red → at most 49

The caps stop a good average from hiding one serious problem.

06 · Cabin SensorsWhy a CO₂ spot sensor on the tablet matters

On Earth, warm breath rises and fresh air mixes in. In microgravity there is no convection, so exhaled carbon dioxide can collect in an invisible pocket around a sleeping or working astronaut's face unless fans keep air moving. The cabin average can look fine while the air at one spot is much worse. A spot sensor on the tablet measures the air right where the astronaut is.

What high CO₂ does

  • Headaches, poorer concentration and slower decisions, which our memory and reaction tests can pick up.
  • Widening of brain blood vessels; researchers think it may add to the eye problem SANS.
  • Usually caused by a scrubber fault, a crowded module or poor ventilation.

Earth air is about 0.3 mmHg CO₂ (≈400 ppm). The app shows mmHg, ppm and %, plus O₂, pressure, temperature and humidity. It shows green below 2.5 mmHg and red above 4 mmHg. Response: move to a ventilated area, increase airflow, check the scrubber, and repeat Test 03.

07 · Radiation dosimeterThe invisible risk, and its aftermath

Outside Earth's magnetic field the crew is hit by galactic cosmic rays all the time and, without much warning, by solar particle events (solar storms). Radiation cannot be seen or felt, so only a dosimeter shows it.

Dose rate (µSv/h) = absorbed dose (µGy/h) × quality factor 3.6

The app tracks dose rate, dose today and career dose against the 600 mSv limit. A dose rate of 417 µSv/h or more turns red and sounds the alarm.

08 · VTE MonitorBlood clots in weightlessness

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) means a blood clot in a vein (thrombosis) that can break off and travel to the lungs (pulmonary embolism). In microgravity, body fluids shift towards the head and blood in the neck (jugular) veins can flow slowly or even backwards. A NASA study of ISS crew found a clot in one astronaut's neck vein and abnormal flow in several others.

Why it matters on the way to Mars

  • A lung clot can be life-threatening within hours, and Earth is minutes to hours away.
  • No CT scanner or hospital on board; only ultrasound and a few blood tests.
  • Blood thinners are limited and raise bleeding risk during spacewalks and injuries.

How the module works

  • Every 15 days (or any time) the astronaut answers 6 questions on leg swelling, pain, warmth and colour change, sudden breathlessness and chest pain: Yes / No / Prefer not to answer.
  • Breathlessness or chest pain → emergency warning and highest-priority simulated transmission to Earth. Other symptoms → Medical Officer review.
  • D-dimer, Doppler ultrasound and blood-count results are entered by medical staff and stored as entered, never interpreted.
  • No diagnosis, no probability and no "healthy" label: the module only flags who needs a doctor's look.

09 · Action Center, Pharmacy & TrendsWhat to do next

  • Action Center: for each non-green system, what we noticed, simple steps first, approved kit medicines with protocol cards, and when to call the Medical Officer.
  • Pharmacy: radiation-adjusted expiry shelf life × 0.8; run-out forecast today + pills left ÷ daily use; allergy blocks; warnings for drowsy medicines, ibuprofen with low fluids, pseudoephedrine with heart alerts, and zolpidem beyond 3 nights.
  • Trends: 30-day graphs with the personal normal band, and the health score compared this week vs last week vs first week.
  • Crew & Buddy: privacy choices (Red is always shared) and an anonymous buddy flag.

10 · Reports to EarthTiny briefs and return readiness

A plain-text brief under 1 KB is queued for the next comms window (delay shown is simulated). Earth-return readiness = Bones 30% (ARED) + Heart 25% + Muscle 20% + Eyes 15% + Radiation 10%: Ready ≥92% · Recondition 80–91% · Medical review <80%.

11 · Limits & honestyWhat this prototype is and is not

  • All astronauts, sensor readings, blood and VTE records are fictional demonstration data.
  • ZERO-GRAM is decision support, not a diagnosis; it does not replace an eye scan, blood tests, ultrasound or a doctor.
  • All thresholds and weights are prototype choices that would need clinical validation. No real Earth communication takes place.
  • Everything runs offline and is saved only on this device.
